Four built — two honest gaps

Measured against RBI's Draft Model Risk Management guidance (24 June 2026). Naming gaps beats hiding them.

Already built (4)
  • Board-approved policy framework — versioned YAML with quarterly review cycle.
  • Live model inventory — unregistered shadow agents fail closed at the gate.
  • Risk tiering & drift detection — ErrRiskChanged stops silent upgrades.
  • Vendor accountability — third-party models cross the exact same gate as internal ones.
Still open — RFCs in repo (2)
  • Operator-triggered AI kill switch (RFC-001) — we have circuit breakers and budget caps, not a clean mid-flight hard stop across active multi-agent trees (pattern reference: CrowdStrike Falcon Guardian).
  • 10-year archive vs. live inventory (RFC-002) — RBI wants decommissioned models kept for 10 years, without polluting the live zero-drift runtime catalog.

Two open problems worth solving right

Neither gap is a one-line flag. Both sit at the seam between live runtime control and long-term audit history.

Read & critique both designs: docs/open-problems/RFC-001-ai-kill-switch.md and RFC-002-ten-year-retention.md.

Check yourself — the three hard questions

The three questions auditors and staff engineers ask most. Have a go before clicking to reveal.

Why not let a second LLM act as the compliance guard?
Click to reveal
Because LLMs are probabilistic. You cannot prove an LLM will block a prompt-injected PII transfer 100% of the time; deterministic Go code fails closed in under 1 ms and produces repeatable audit proof.
Why did var_calculator pass a standard approval check?
Click to reveal
Its status was still approved and unexpired — nobody rejected it. Only comparing the approved risk tier (medium) against the live declared tier (high) catches silent drift.
Why isn't an encrypted database enough for an audit trail?
Click to reveal
Encryption hides rows from outsiders, not from an insider who holds database credentials. Hash chaining plus a pinned-key Ed25519 signature catches edits made by your own team.

One rulebook, five jurisdictions — not one fragile server

One versioned YAML in Git drives India, ADGM, DIFC, SAMA, and Europe across stateless replicas behind your load balancer.

examples/policy.yamlRBI Rec 14 / EU Art. 17
version: "2026.11"
approved_by: "board-risk-committee@bank"
policies:
  residency:
    enforce_per_message: true
    pii_allowed_regions: ["in", "adgm", "difc", "sama"]
    deny_regions: ["us"]
  consent:
    ledger_mode: "append-only"
  approval_drift:
    block_on_risk_class_change: true
  disclosure:
    emit_on_first_event: true
Why one gate does not mean one single point of failure
  • Stateless horizontal scale: run 10 or 100 identical gate replicas behind your ingress; every replica loads the same Git-signed policy.yaml.
  • Zero country forks: residency is checked per message envelope, so a single binary serves India, ADGM, DIFC, SAMA, and EU clients safely.
  • Auditable policy diffs: every regulatory update is a reviewed pull request with a commit hash stamped into the audit log.
